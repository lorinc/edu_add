## Part 1. Inside the Earth

Nobody has ever been deeper than a few kilometres into the Earth. The deepest hole ever drilled, the Kola Superdeep Borehole in northern Russia, reached about 12 km. The centre of the Earth is about 6,370 km down. So the hole scratched less than 0.2% of the way.

Yet we know quite a lot about what's down there. How?

### Listening to earthquakes

When an earthquake happens, it sends **seismic waves** through the whole planet. Instruments called **seismometers**, all over the world, record when the waves arrive and how strong they are.

There are two main kinds of waves that travel through the inside of the Earth:

- **P-waves** (primary waves) squeeze and stretch the rock in the direction they travel, like a push along a spring. They are the fastest, so they arrive first. They can travel through solids, liquids and gases.
- **S-waves** (secondary waves) shake the rock from side to side, like a wave sent along a rope. They are slower. They **cannot travel through liquid**.

Here's the clever part. On the side of the Earth opposite a big earthquake, seismometers pick up P-waves but **no S-waves**. There's a huge "S-wave shadow". Something in the middle of the Earth stops them. Since S-waves can't pass through liquid, that something must be liquid.

Waves also bend when they pass from one material into another, the way a straw looks bent in a glass of water. By measuring exactly where and when waves arrive, scientists worked out where the boundaries between layers are, and roughly what each layer is made of. It's a bit like working out what's inside a wrapped present by tapping it and listening.

### The layers

From the outside in:

| Layer | Depth | State | Made of |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Crust** | 0 to 5-70 km | Solid | Light rocks. Thin under oceans (about 5-10 km), thick under continents (30-70 km). |
| **Mantle** | to about 2,900 km | Solid, but can flow very slowly | Hot, dense rock |
| **Outer core** | to about 5,150 km | **Liquid** | Mostly iron and nickel |
| **Inner core** | to the centre, about 6,370 km | Solid | Mostly iron and nickel |

Two things in this table surprise most people.

**The mantle is solid, but it flows.** Think of a glacier: it's solid ice, but over years it slides downhill. Or think of very cold honey. Mantle rock is so hot and under so much pressure that, over millions of years, it creeps. It moves a few centimetres a year.

**The inner core is solid even though it's hotter than the outer core.** The inner core is about 5,000 °C or more, about as hot as the surface of the Sun. It should melt. But the pressure at the centre of the Earth is so enormous that the atoms are squeezed together into a solid anyway.

### Plates: the part that moves

The top of the mantle and the crust together form a rigid, brittle shell called the **lithosphere** (from Greek *lithos*, stone). It's about 100 km thick. Underneath, the upper mantle is hotter and softer: this layer is the **asthenosphere** (Greek *asthenes*, weak).

The lithosphere isn't one piece. It's broken into about a dozen large pieces and many small ones, called **tectonic plates**. They rest on the soft asthenosphere and move, very slowly, relative to each other.

The movement is a few centimetres a year: about as fast as your fingernails grow. That sounds like nothing. But in 10 million years, 3 cm a year adds up to 300 km. In 200 million years, it opens an ocean.

### Why the Earth is hot inside

Two reasons:
1. **Leftover heat** from when the Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago, from rocks crashing together.
2. **Radioactivity:** radioactive elements in the rocks (such as uranium and potassium) slowly decay and release heat.

This heat is the engine that moves the plates. Everything in the next four parts — drifting continents, earthquakes, volcanoes — is driven by it.

## Part 2. The idea nobody believed

In 1912, a German scientist called **Alfred Wegener** proposed something outrageous: the continents move.

He wasn't the first to notice that South America and Africa fit together like jigsaw pieces. Anyone with a world map can see it. But Wegener collected evidence that went far beyond the shape of coastlines:

- **Fossils:** the remains of *Mesosaurus*, a small freshwater reptile, are found in Brazil and in southern Africa, and nowhere else. It couldn't have swum across an ocean. The fossil fern *Glossopteris* is found in South America, Africa, India, Antarctica and Australia.
- **Rocks:** mountain ranges and rock layers of the same type and age line up across the Atlantic, as if a piece of paper had been torn in two.
- **Ancient climate:** there are scratches left by glaciers in places that are now hot, like India and southern Africa, and coal (made from tropical forests) under Antarctica.

Wegener's explanation: around 300 million years ago, all the continents were joined in one supercontinent. He called it **Pangaea** ("all lands"). Since then, it has broken apart and the pieces have drifted.

### Why he was rejected

Most geologists, especially in Britain and America, rejected the idea, often with mockery. One reason was fair: Wegener could not explain **what force** could push whole continents through the solid ocean floor. His suggested forces were far too weak, and physicists showed it.

But the other reasons were less scientific. Wegener was a meteorologist (a weather scientist), not a geologist: an outsider. Accepting his idea meant that experts would have to admit that the textbooks they wrote and taught were wrong. Geologists invented other explanations for the fossils instead, like long "land bridges" across oceans that had since sunk.

Wegener died in 1930 on an expedition across the Greenland ice sheet. His idea was mostly ignored for 30 years.

### The evidence from the sea floor

After the Second World War, ships used **sonar** (sound echoes) to map the ocean floor, partly to hunt submarines. The maps showed something nobody expected.

**Marie Tharp**, an American geologist and map-maker, plotted the data in the early 1950s. She found a long underwater mountain range running down the middle of the Atlantic, with a deep valley (a rift) along its centre. It turned out to be part of a chain of ridges circling the whole planet, about 65,000 km long. Her boss, Bruce Heezen, first dismissed her finding as "girl talk"; he came round when the earthquake data matched it.

Then came magnetic stripes. Every few hundred thousand years, the Earth's magnetic field flips: north becomes south. When lava cools, tiny magnetic minerals in it lock in the direction of the field at that moment. Scientists towing magnetometers behind ships found stripes of normal and reversed magnetism on the sea floor, running parallel to the ridge. And the pattern on one side of the ridge was a **mirror image** of the pattern on the other side.

The explanation, put together in the early 1960s by Harry Hess and others: **sea-floor spreading**. New ocean floor is made at the ridges, where hot rock rises and cools. It then moves away on both sides, like two conveyor belts. The stripes are a recording of the magnetic field over millions of years, like a tape.

Wegener had been right that continents move. He'd been wrong about how: continents don't plough through the ocean floor. The whole lithosphere moves, ocean floor and continents together. By the end of the 1960s, the new theory of **plate tectonics** was accepted almost everywhere.

### What this story teaches

Wegener had good evidence and a weak mechanism. His critics had a fair objection, and a lot of unfair reasons. The idea won when new evidence became overwhelming, not because the old experts changed their minds: many of them never did. A famous saying (paraphrasing the physicist Max Planck): science advances one funeral at a time.

## Part 3. Plate boundaries

Plates move relative to each other. The interesting things — mountains, earthquakes, volcanoes, ocean trenches — happen where two plates meet: at **plate boundaries**. There are three main kinds.

### 1. Divergent boundaries: plates move apart

Hot mantle rock rises, melts partly as the pressure on it drops, and fills the gap with new crust.

- **Under the ocean:** this is a **mid-ocean ridge**, like the Mid-Atlantic Ridge from Part 2. The Atlantic is getting wider by about 2-5 cm a year.
- **On land:** the land stretches and cracks, forming a **rift valley**. The East African Rift is slowly splitting Africa; in millions of years it may become a new ocean.
- **Iceland** is a place where the Mid-Atlantic Ridge rises above the sea. You can walk in the valley between the North American and Eurasian plates at Þingvellir.

Earthquakes here are usually small to moderate. Volcanoes are common, with runny lava.

### 2. Convergent boundaries: plates move towards each other

When two plates collide, what happens depends on what kind of crust they carry. Ocean crust is **denser** (heavier for its size) than continental crust, because it's made of different rock. That matters.

**Ocean meets continent (subduction).** The denser ocean plate bends and sinks under the continent, back into the mantle. This sinking is called **subduction**. Where it goes down, a deep **ocean trench** forms. As the sinking plate heats up, water squeezed out of it helps melt the mantle above, and the melted rock rises to form a line of volcanoes on the continent. The Andes in South America formed this way.

**Ocean meets ocean.** The older, colder, denser plate sinks under the other. This makes a trench and a curved line of volcanic islands. Japan formed this way. The **Mariana Trench** in the western Pacific, at about 11 km deep, is the deepest place on Earth: deeper than Mount Everest is high.

**Continent meets continent.** Neither is dense enough to sink. They crumple and pile up into huge mountain ranges. India crashed into Asia about 50 million years ago and is still pushing north. The result is the **Himalaya**, including Everest, which is still rising by a few millimetres a year. Rocks near the top of Everest contain fossils of sea creatures: they were once on the floor of an ocean that closed.

Convergent boundaries have the world's biggest earthquakes, and the most explosive volcanoes.

### 3. Transform boundaries: plates slide past each other

The plates grind sideways. No crust is made or destroyed. The edges lock together, stress builds up for years or centuries, and then they slip suddenly: an earthquake.

The best-known example is the **San Andreas Fault** in California, where the Pacific plate slides north-west past the North American plate. Los Angeles (on the Pacific plate) is slowly moving towards San Francisco (on the North American plate), at about 5 cm a year.

### What moves the plates?

This was the question Wegener couldn't answer. Today scientists think:
- **Slab pull** is the strongest force: a cold, heavy plate sinking at a subduction zone drags the rest of the plate behind it, like a tablecloth sliding off a table.
- **Ridge push:** new crust at a ridge sits high and slides down and away from it.
- **Mantle convection:** heat from below makes the mantle slowly circulate, hot rock rising and cooler rock sinking.

Scientists still argue about how much each one matters. That's normal: science is a map with some blank areas, and the blank areas are where the interesting work is.

## Part 4. Earthquakes

### What happens

Rocks at a plate boundary are pushed in opposite directions, but friction locks them together. They bend, a little, like a stick you're slowly bending. Energy is stored in the bent rock. When the stress is greater than the friction, the rock suddenly breaks and snaps back: the stored energy is released as seismic waves. This is called **elastic rebound**.

- The **focus** (or hypocentre) is the point underground where the rock breaks.
- The **epicentre** is the point on the surface directly above the focus.
- A **fault** is the crack along which the rocks move.

Most earthquakes happen at plate boundaries. A map of earthquakes is almost a map of the plate edges.

### How big?

Two different measurements, often confused:

- **Magnitude** measures the **energy released** by the earthquake. There's one number per earthquake. Scientists today use the *moment magnitude* scale (the old Richter scale was similar). It's not like a ruler: each step of 1 means about **32 times more energy**. So a magnitude 8 releases about 1,000 times more energy than a magnitude 6 (32 × 32 ≈ 1,000).
- **Intensity** measures **what the shaking did** in one particular place: did people feel it, did plates fall off shelves, did buildings collapse? The same earthquake has high intensity near the epicentre and low intensity far away.

### Same size, different disaster

Here are three real earthquakes.

| Earthquake | Magnitude | Deaths |
|---|---|---|
| Haiti, January 2010 | 7.0 | Estimates range from about 100,000 to over 300,000 |
| Chile, February 2010 | 8.8 | About 500 |
| Japan (Tōhoku), March 2011 | 9.0 | About 18,000, almost all from the tsunami |

The Chilean earthquake released roughly 500 times more energy than the Haitian one. Yet far fewer people died. Why?

- **Buildings:** Chile has strict building codes, enforced after earlier earthquakes, so buildings bent instead of collapsing. In Haiti, most buildings were concrete without enough steel reinforcement, built without inspection.
- **Location:** the Haiti earthquake's focus was shallow and very close to the capital, Port-au-Prince, a crowded city of millions.
- **Wealth and government:** Haiti was the poorest country in the Americas, with a weak government. Historians argue about why: some stress outside causes (a huge debt France forced on Haiti after independence, a US occupation from 1915 to 1934), others inside causes (dictatorship, corruption). Probably both. Rescue and hospitals were overwhelmed.
- **Practice:** Chileans and Japanese do earthquake drills from childhood.

Notice also that the death toll in Haiti isn't one number. The Haitian government said over 300,000; other studies estimated much lower. Counting deaths in a chaotic disaster is hard, and different groups have reasons to count high or low (for example, to attract aid, or to make the response look better). When you read a number, ask who counted and how.

Scientists summarise this as: **risk = hazard × vulnerability**. The hazard is the earthquake. Vulnerability is how exposed and unprepared people are. The earthquake is natural. The disaster is partly man-made.

### Tsunamis

When an undersea earthquake suddenly lifts or drops the sea floor, the whole column of water above moves. This makes a **tsunami**: a series of waves that can cross a whole ocean at the speed of a jet plane. In deep water the wave is low, and a ship might not notice it. As it reaches shallow coast, it slows down and piles up, sometimes over 10 metres high, and floods far inland.

One warning sign: the sea suddenly pulls back from the beach, exposing the sea floor. People sometimes walk out to look. The right reaction is to run uphill immediately.

### Can we predict them?

No. Scientists can say *where* big earthquakes are likely (along known faults) and roughly *how often* (for example, "a 70% chance of a large earthquake here in the next 30 years"). Nobody can say *when*: which day, or even which year. What works is preparation: building codes, drills, warning systems that give a few seconds to a minute of warning after the quake starts, and tsunami warnings.

## Part 5. Volcanoes, and why people live on plate boundaries

### How volcanoes form

A volcano is an opening where **magma** (melted rock underground) reaches the surface. Once it's out, it's called **lava**. Most volcanoes are at plate boundaries:

- At **divergent** boundaries, rock melts because the pressure on rising mantle drops.
- At **subduction** zones, water from the sinking plate lowers the melting point of the mantle above.

Some are in the middle of plates, above **hotspots**: places where unusually hot material rises from deep in the mantle. The plate moves over the hotspot, so a chain of volcanoes forms, oldest at one end. The Hawaiian islands are one: the Big Island is the youngest and still active; the islands to the north-west are older, eroded and extinct.

### Two kinds of eruption

What makes a volcano gentle or explosive is mainly how **runny** (or sticky) the magma is. Scientists call stickiness **viscosity**.

- **Runny magma** (low in silica, a mineral made from silicon and oxygen) lets gas bubble out easily. Lava flows in rivers. These eruptions build wide, gently sloping **shield volcanoes**, like those of Hawaii. They're dangerous to buildings in the lava's path, but people can usually walk away.
- **Sticky magma** (high in silica) traps gas, like a shaken bottle with the cap on. Pressure builds, then the volcano explodes. These build steep **composite volcanoes** (stratovolcanoes) from layers of ash and lava, like Mount Fuji, Mount St Helens or Vesuvius. They're typical of subduction zones.

The most dangerous thing in an explosive eruption is often not lava, but a **pyroclastic flow**: an avalanche of hot gas, ash and rock rushing downhill at over 100 km/h, at hundreds of degrees. That is what destroyed Pompeii when Vesuvius erupted in 79 CE.

### So why do people live there?

Hundreds of millions of people live near active volcanoes or on major faults. Cities like Tokyo, Istanbul, Lima, Mexico City, Naples, San Francisco and Jakarta sit in danger zones. Is that a crazy choice?

Reasons people stay:

1. **Fertile soil.** Volcanic ash breaks down into soil rich in minerals. The Indonesian island of Java, covered in volcanoes, is one of the most densely populated places on Earth, largely because its soil feeds so many people.
2. **Energy.** Iceland heats about nine out of ten homes with geothermal heat, and makes a large share of its electricity from it.
3. **Jobs and money:** tourism (hot springs, volcano tours), mining (volcanic areas concentrate metals like copper and gold), and ports.
4. **The city was there first.** Many cities are centuries old. People live where their families, jobs and lives are.
5. **Rare events feel unreal.** A big eruption or earthquake may come once in several lifetimes. People weigh a danger that might happen against a job, a house and a family that are here now.
6. **No choice.** Many people simply can't afford to move.

Ways to live with the risk instead of leaving: monitoring volcanoes (measuring small earthquakes, ground swelling and gases before eruptions, which *can* often give days to weeks of warning, unlike earthquakes), evacuation plans, hazard maps that restrict building in the worst zones, and strong buildings.

So is it crazy? It depends on how you weigh a rare, huge danger against everyday, certain benefits. This question doesn't have one right answer. It has better and worse arguments.
