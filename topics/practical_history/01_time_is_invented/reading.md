# Time is invented

Practical history topic. Six reading parts and four task sessions.

Big idea: the sun and the seasons are natural. Hours, weeks, time zones and punctuality are **human inventions**. Each was made by someone, for a reason, and usually served someone's interests. That means they could be different, and that you can choose how you use your own time.

How to read: read the part once; read again with your Google Doc open and write the 3-5 key ideas in your own words; then answer the day's questions without looking back at the text.

## Part 1. Why 60 minutes, why 24 hours?

### Counting in sixties

Around 5,000 years ago, the Sumerians, in what is now Iraq, counted in groups of 60. The Babylonians, who came after them, kept this system for mathematics and astronomy. We still use it, without noticing, every time we look at a clock: 60 seconds in a minute, 60 minutes in an hour. Also 360 degrees in a circle (6 × 60).

Why 60? Nobody knows for sure. A good guess: 60 can be divided evenly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30. That makes fractions easy: a third of 60 is 20, a quarter is 15. Try that with 100: a third of 100 is 33.333...

### Why 24 hours?

The ancient Egyptians divided the night into 12 parts, using stars that rose one after another through the night. They divided the daytime into 12 parts too, using sundials. 12 + 12 = 24.

But here is the surprise: **those hours were not the same length.** Daytime always had 12 hours, whether it was summer or winter. So a summer daytime hour was long and a winter one short. These are called **seasonal hours** (or temporal hours). For most people, for most of history, that was perfectly sensible: you work while it's light, and the light is what you divide.

Greek astronomers (around 150 BCE) used 24 *equal* hours for their calculations, but ordinary life went on with seasonal hours. Equal hours only became normal in Europe in the 1300s, when mechanical clocks appeared, because a machine ticks at the same speed in summer and in winter. The machine changed the hour, not the other way round.

### Minutes and seconds

For most of history, almost nobody needed minutes. Clocks only got a minute hand after the pendulum clock (1656) made them accurate enough. Before that, a good clock could be wrong by a quarter of an hour a day. Seconds only mattered to scientists and navigators. Today your phone measures time to a fraction of a second and is corrected by atomic clocks.

Ask yourself: what in your day actually needs minute-level precision? What would happen if you used seasonal hours?

## Part 2. The week, and the weeks that failed

### Seven days

A day comes from the Earth turning. A month roughly comes from the Moon. A year comes from the Earth going round the Sun. **The week comes from nowhere in nature.** It's purely human.

The seven-day week has two roots:

1. **The Jewish Sabbath:** in the Bible, God creates the world in six days and rests on the seventh. Jewish communities kept a strict seven-day cycle with a rest day for thousands of years. Christianity and later Islam inherited it.
2. **The seven "planets":** ancient astronomers saw seven bodies moving across the sky against the stars: the Sun, the Moon, Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn. Astrologers in the Greek and Roman world gave each day to one of them.

You still speak this history every day:

| English | Origin | Spanish | Origin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sunday | the Sun | domingo | *dominicus*, the Lord's day |
| Monday | the Moon | lunes | the Moon (*luna*) |
| Tuesday | Tiw, Germanic war god | martes | Mars, Roman war god |
| Wednesday | Woden (Odin) | miércoles | Mercury |
| Thursday | Thor | jueves | Jupiter |
| Friday | Frigg, Germanic goddess | viernes | Venus |
| Saturday | Saturn | sábado | the Sabbath |

The English names swapped Roman gods for similar Germanic ones: Mars, the war god, became Tiw, a war god; Jupiter, who throws thunderbolts, became Thor, the thunder god.

The Romans had used an eight-day market cycle before. In 321 CE, the Roman emperor Constantine, who had become Christian, made Sunday a day of rest. The seven-day week became law across the empire.

### The weeks that failed

Twice in modern history, a revolutionary government tried to abolish the week. Both times, they wanted to break the power of the church and make people think of time in a new, rational way.

**France, 1793.** The French Revolution created a new calendar: 12 months of 30 days, each split into three "weeks" of **10 days** (called *décades*), plus 5 or 6 holidays at the end of the year. Days had new names, like "primidi", "duodi"... Months were named after the weather: *Brumaire* (the foggy month), *Thermidor* (the hot month). For about a year and a half they even used **decimal time**: 10 hours a day, 100 minutes an hour, and clocks were made with 10-hour faces.

It failed. Workers got one rest day in ten instead of one in seven, and they hated it. People kept their Sundays. Decimal time was dropped in 1795. Napoleon abolished the whole calendar at the start of 1806.

**The Soviet Union, 1929.** Stalin's government introduced the "continuous working week": 5 days, 4 of work and 1 of rest. Workers were split into groups with different rest days, so the factories never stopped. The result: family members often had different days off and couldn't spend them together. Church on Sunday became impossible. In 1931 it was changed to a 6-day week, and in 1940 the Soviet Union went back to 7 days.

Both experiments show something: a calendar isn't just a way of counting. It shapes when families meet, when people pray, when they rest, and who controls their time. That's why people fight over it.

## Part 3. Clocks and bells

### Before clocks

People told time by the sun (sundials), by water dripping from a container at a steady rate (water clocks), by candles marked in sections, and by sand in hourglasses. In 1088, the Chinese engineer **Su Song** built a giant astronomical clock tower in Kaifeng, powered by a water wheel, which struck the hours with bells and drums. It was a marvel, but it was a machine for the emperor and his astronomers, not for ordinary people.

### Prayer time

In Europe, the first mechanical clocks appeared at the end of the 1200s. One of the first places that needed them was the **monastery**. Monks prayed at fixed times through the day and night, the "canonical hours". Someone had to wake everyone in the dark for night prayers. A machine that rang a bell at the right time was very useful.

The word "clock" probably comes from medieval words for **bell** (Latin *clocca*, French *cloche*). Early clocks often had no face at all: they didn't show the time, they *rang* it.

### The town clock

During the 1300s, towns across Europe built clock towers. Merchants and town councils paid for them. Bells now marked the opening of markets, the start and end of the working day for craftsmen, and the curfew (from French *couvre-feu*, "cover the fire": the bell that told people to put out their fires and go home at night).

Notice the shift: time was moving from **God's time** (the church calendar, prayer hours) to **merchants' time** (markets, work hours, wages paid by the day). Whoever controls the bell controls when people start and stop.

### Accuracy

Early mechanical clocks were bad: off by 15 minutes or more a day. In 1656, the Dutch scientist **Christiaan Huygens** built the first pendulum clock, accurate to within about 15 *seconds* a day. That's when minute hands became worth adding.

In the 1700s, an English carpenter and clockmaker, **John Harrison**, built clocks that kept precise time even on a rocking ship. This was a matter of life and death: to know how far east or west a ship was (its longitude), sailors had to know the exact time at home and compare it with the local time from the sun. Bad clocks meant shipwrecks. Harrison's work took decades, and the experts on the prize committee, mostly astronomers who favoured their own method, made him wait years for his reward.

## Part 4. Railway time

### Every town its own noon

Until the 1800s, every town set its clocks by its own sun: noon was when the sun was highest *there*. That's called **local time**. Because the Earth turns, the sun is highest a bit later the further west you go: about 4 minutes later for each degree of longitude.

So Bristol, in the west of England, was about **10 minutes behind London**. Nobody cared: a horse-drawn coach took a day to get between them.

### Then came the train

Trains made those 10 minutes a problem. A train timetable has to say when the train leaves and arrives. Whose time? If every station uses its own local time, the timetable becomes a puzzle, and trains running on the same track can crash.

In **1840**, the Great Western Railway started using London time at all its stations. Other railways followed, and by the end of the 1840s most of Britain's railways used London time, set by the Royal Observatory at **Greenwich**. It became known as **railway time**.

Many towns resisted. For years, some kept two times. The Corn Exchange in Bristol still has a clock with **two minute hands**: one for London time and one for Bristol time. Parliament only made Greenwich time the legal time for all of Britain in **1880**.

### The day of two noons

The United States was much bigger. In the 1870s there were dozens of different railway times, and every city had its own local time too. On **18 November 1883**, the railway companies themselves — not the government — switched the whole country to four time zones. Clocks across the country were reset; in many places, noon struck twice that day. It was called "the day of two noons". The US Congress only made time zones law in 1918, 35 years later.

Who decided what time it was in America? Private companies, for their own business. Everyone else followed.

### The whole world

In 1884, delegates from 25 countries met in Washington and agreed that the line through Greenwich would be the **prime meridian**: 0° longitude, the starting line for time zones around the world. Britain was then the world's biggest sea power, and most of the world's sea charts already used Greenwich. France refused to vote for it (it abstained), and kept using Paris time until 1911.

That's why the world's clock (Greenwich Mean Time, today UTC) starts in a suburb of London.

## Part 5. Time is money

### Two ways to work

Think about how a farmer or a fisherman works. The cows need milking when they need milking. The boat goes out when the tide is right. Work follows the **task**: you work until it's done, sometimes all day and night at harvest, sometimes lightly for days. This is called **task orientation**.

Now think about a factory. Hundreds of workers have to start at the same minute, because the machines run together and stopping them costs money. The worker is paid for **hours**, not for finished tasks. The employer is buying your time.

### The historian's argument

In 1967, the British historian **E. P. Thompson** wrote a famous essay about how this change happened in England during the Industrial Revolution (roughly 1760-1850). His argument:

- Before factories, many workers set their own pace. Weavers working at home often took Monday off (they jokingly called it "Saint Monday"), then worked late into the night at the end of the week to finish.
- Factories needed **time discipline**: bells, clocks, fines for being late, locked gates. Children in factories were taught that wasting time was a sin.
- Schools, churches and writers taught that **punctuality and hard work were moral virtues**, and idleness a vice. The American writer Benjamin Franklin put it in three words in 1748: "time is money."
- Workers sometimes said employers cheated with the clock. One factory worker's memoir from the 1800s claimed the clocks were set forward in the morning and back at night, so workers did more hours than they were paid for. Workers weren't allowed to carry watches.
- Over time, workers learned the new rules, and then fought **within** them: not against the clock, but for fewer hours. Hence the campaigns for the 10-hour day, then the 8-hour day.

### The other side

Other historians argue that Thompson exaggerated:
- Plenty of people before factories cared a lot about time: monks, merchants, sailors, people who went to market. Watches were already common among working people in 18th-century England, according to records of stolen goods and wills.
- Task work wasn't paradise: farm work was often exhausting and controlled by landlords, and "working until it's done" could mean all night.
- Clock discipline brought benefits too: fixed hours made it possible to have a fixed time *off*.

Both sides agree on the main point: in the industrial age, **time became something you sell**, and the clock became a tool of control. They disagree about how sudden and how harsh the change was.

### Why this matters to you

School bells, timetables, being marked late, being "on time" as a sign of good character: many of these habits come from the same era. That doesn't make them bad. Being on time is genuinely useful when people depend on each other. But it's worth knowing that punctuality is something we learned, not something we were born with.

## Part 6. Other clocks: tasks, bodies and politics

### Measuring time by tasks

Many societies measured time by things everyone knew:
- In Madagascar, a short time could be described as "a rice-cooking" (about half an hour) or "the frying of a locust" (a moment).
- In 17th-century Chile, an earthquake was described as lasting "two creeds": the time it takes to say a well-known prayer twice.
- The Nuer, cattle herders in South Sudan studied by an anthropologist in the 1930s, organised the day around cattle work: the time of milking, of taking the herd to pasture. They didn't think of time as something that could be "wasted", because they didn't treat it as a thing at all.

### Your body's clock

Your body has its own clock: the **circadian rhythm**, a roughly 24-hour cycle set by light. It decides when you feel sleepy and when alert.

Here's a fact that concerns you directly: during puberty, the body clock shifts **later**. Teenagers' brains release the sleep hormone melatonin about two hours later than children's or adults'. So many teenagers genuinely can't fall asleep early, and genuinely struggle to wake early. It's biology, not just laziness. (Screens at night make it worse: their light delays melatonin further.)

Because of this, the American Academy of Pediatrics recommended in 2014 that secondary schools start no earlier than 8:30. California made this law for high schools in 2022. Most schools still start earlier: the timetable suits buses, parents' work and sports, not teenage brains. Whose needs should the clock serve?

### Political time

Clock time is also decided by governments, sometimes for odd reasons:
- **Daylight saving time** (moving clocks forward in summer) was first introduced by Germany and Austria-Hungary in 1916, during the First World War, to save coal used for lighting. Britain copied it weeks later. In 2019, the European Parliament voted to stop changing the clocks; countries still haven't agreed how, so the clocks still change.
- **Spain** is geographically in line with Britain, but uses the same time as Germany. In 1940, the dictator Franco moved Spain's clocks forward an hour to match Nazi Germany, and it was never changed back. Some say this is part of why Spaniards eat lunch and dinner so late by the clock; others say the late meal times are older than that. A good example of an attractive explanation that might be only half true.
- **China** is about as wide as the continental US, but the whole country has used one time zone, Beijing time, since 1949, as a symbol of national unity. In the far west, in Xinjiang, the sun can rise around 10 a.m. in winter. Many local Uyghur people use an unofficial local time, two hours behind. Which clock you use there can be a political statement.

### So what?

Hours, weeks, time zones, punctuality, school start times: all were chosen by someone, for some reason, often to serve whoever had power: priests, merchants, railways, factory owners, states. That doesn't mean they're all bad. It means they're choices. And your own time, outside the agreements you've made with other people, is yours to organise.
