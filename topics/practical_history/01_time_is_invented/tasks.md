# Time is invented: task sessions

Three task sessions (tasks 1, 2 and 4) and one optional task (3). Each produces something in your Google Doc.

## Task 1. Your time audit

You've been writing time estimates and actual times on your day sheet every day. Now look at them like a scientist.

1. Copy into your doc a table of every block from your day sheets so far: block, estimate, actual, difference.
2. Answer:
   - On average, do you overestimate or underestimate? By how much?
   - Which subject do you misjudge most? Why do you think that is?
   - Was there a block where time "flew" or "dragged"? What were you doing?
3. **The 60-second test:** close your eyes, and say "now" when you think 60 seconds have passed. Someone else (or a phone timer you can't see) times you. Do it three times. Then do it three more times while doing something (writing your name over and over, or reading). Record all six results.
4. Write 5-8 sentences: what did you learn about your own sense of time, and what will you change in your estimates next week?

Done: the table, the six 60-second results, the written answers.

## Task 2. Source detective: is time money?

Here is a real source. Read it twice.

> "Remember that time is money. He that can earn ten shillings a day by his labour, and goes abroad, or sits idle, one half of that day, though he spends but sixpence during his diversion or idleness, ought not to reckon that the only expense; he has really spent, or rather thrown away, five shillings besides."
>
> Benjamin Franklin, *Advice to a Young Tradesman*, 1748.

In plain words: if you could earn money in that time, then every hour you spend relaxing has cost you the money you didn't earn.

Answer the six source detective questions in your doc, in full sentences:

1. Who made this, when, and for whom? (Franklin was a printer, businessman and writer; the text is addressed to a young man starting a business.)
2. What does he want the reader to think or do?
3. What is left out, and who doesn't get to speak? (Think: people who don't sell their time, children, people who can't find work, people who care for others.)
4. Who benefits if people believe it?
5. What would the other side say? Use Part 5 and Part 6 of the reading.
6. What evidence would change your mind?

Then: is "time is money" true for **you**, right now? Where is it true and where isn't it? 5-8 sentences.

Done: six answers plus your own judgement.

## Task 3 (optional, weekend or group challenge). Design a calendar

Work with your sibling if you both agree to; otherwise alone. If together: one shared doc, and each of you writes your own final paragraph.

Design a new way to organise time for a society. It can be a week of a different length, a different day (seasonal hours? no hours?), different rest days, different school hours, different time zones.

1. Describe your system in a few bullet points.
2. Who benefits from it? Who loses? (Workers, employers, families, schools, religious groups, teenagers, farmers...)
3. Why did the French 10-day week and the Soviet 5-day week fail? Would yours fail for the same reasons?
4. What would it take to actually make people switch? Who has the power to do it?

Done: description, the winners and losers, the comparison with the two failed weeks, and the "how to make it happen" answer.

## Task 4. Final piece: who decides what time it is?

Write 400-600 words answering the question: **Who decides what time it is?**

Use at least four examples from different parts of the topic (for example: Babylon, monasteries, railways, factories, Spain, China, school start times). Your piece should:
- explain at least two different kinds of power behind time (religious, business, state, scientific),
- include one argument you **disagree** with, put as strongly as you can, and your answer to it,
- end with what this means for how **you** use your own time.

Plan for 5 minutes (bullet points), write, then reread once and fix three things. Typed by you, no tools.

Done: the piece, with a word count at the end.

## Weekend (optional)

Spend one day (or half a day) organised by tasks instead of the clock: no clock-checking; eat when hungry, stop when the task is done. Afterwards, write three lines: what was easier, what was harder, what surprised you.
