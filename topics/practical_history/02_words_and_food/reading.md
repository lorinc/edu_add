# Words and food are fossils

Practical history topic. Six reading parts and four task sessions.

Big idea: a fossil is a trace of something long gone, preserved in rock. Everyday words and dishes are fossils too. They preserve centuries of contact, trade, conquest and power that often nobody wrote down as history. Once you know how to read them, you'll see history on every menu and in every sentence.

How to read: read the part once; read again with your Google Doc open and write the 3-5 key ideas in your own words; then answer the day's questions without looking back.

---

## Part 1. How words travel

### Borrowing

Languages borrow words from each other all the time. Linguists call them **loanwords** (they are never given back). Words get borrowed for a few reasons:

1. **A new thing arrives with its name.** Coffee, tomato, kangaroo, pizza, emoji: the thing came from somewhere, and its name came with it.
2. **Prestige.** People copy the language of those with power, money or fashion. Today many languages borrow English words (*weekend*, *download*, *cool*). For centuries, English borrowed French words for the same reason.
3. **Conquest and living together.** When two groups share a land, words cross over, especially from the powerful group to the other.

So a loanword tells you that two groups of people met, and often **which one had power**.

### English, a mixed language

English began as the language of Germanic tribes (Angles, Saxons, Jutes) who settled in Britain from around 450 CE. Then it was hit by wave after wave of other languages:
- **Latin**, through the Church, from about 600 CE.
- **Old Norse**, from Viking invaders and settlers, from about 800.
- **French**, after the Norman conquest of 1066, for about 300 years the language of England's rulers.
- **Latin and Greek again**, through scholars and scientists from the 1500s.
- And then words from everywhere English speakers traded, ruled and settled.

If you count all the words in a big English dictionary, only about a quarter come from the old Germanic core. More than half come from French and Latin. But if you count the words people **use most** — *the, a, is, have, go, eat, house, mother, water, good* — almost all are Germanic. The old core is small but carries the everyday weight; the borrowed words pile up in the formal, technical, legal and fancy parts of the language.

### How to read a word's history

An **etymology** is the history of a word. A good free tool: **Etymonline** (https://www.etymonline.com). Type a word, and it shows where it came from, step by step, with dates.

Example: *salary*. From Latin *salarium*, connected to *sal*, salt. A popular story says Roman soldiers were paid in salt. Historians say there's no good evidence for that; nobody is sure exactly why the word links to salt. A good etymology tells you what's known and what's only a story. Watch for that difference all through this topic.

## Part 2. 1066: cows in English, beef in French

### The conquest

In 1066, William, Duke of Normandy, invaded England and won the Battle of Hastings. For the next 300 years, England's kings, nobles, judges and bishops spoke **French**. The ordinary people kept speaking **English**. Gradually, English swallowed thousands of French words.

### The animal and the meat

Here is the famous pattern:

| Animal (English) | Meat (French origin) | French today |
|---|---|---|
| cow | beef | *bœuf* |
| pig, swine | pork | *porc* |
| sheep | mutton | *mouton* |
| calf | veal | *veau* |
| deer | venison | *venaison* |

The explanation often given: English-speaking peasants raised and looked after the animals, so they named them in English. French-speaking lords only met the animals cooked, on the table, so the meat got French names.

### Is it true?

This story was made famous by a **novel**: Walter Scott's *Ivanhoe* (1819), in which a jester explains it to a swineherd. Linguists say the pattern is real, but the story is too neat:
- In medieval English, "beef" and "mutton" were also used for the **living animals**. The split into animal/meat took centuries.
- The French words came in through many routes, not just the dinner table: cooks, markets, menus written in French for centuries after the conquest.
- Some meats never split: we eat *chicken* (English) and *fish* (English).

Still, the bigger point stands: the French words cluster where power was.

### The language of power

Look at which words came from French:
- **Government:** government, parliament, crown, state, nation, council, minister, tax.
- **Law:** court, judge, jury, justice, crime, prison, sentence, verdict, attorney.
- **War:** army, soldier, battle, enemy, guard, siege.
- **Fine living:** dinner, feast, fashion, jewel, palace, castle, cuisine.

And which stayed English: **house, home, bread, work, field, plough, hand, eat, sleep, child, love**.

English often has **three** words for one idea, from three layers: English (simple, warm), French (formal), Latin (technical, learned).

| English | French | Latin |
|---|---|---|
| ask | question | interrogate |
| kingly | royal | regal |
| rise | mount | ascend |
| fear | terror | trepidation |
| time | age | epoch |

Even today, the plain word feels honest and close, the Latin one official and distant. Politicians and companies know this: compare "we're sacking 200 people" with "the company is implementing workforce optimisation".

### The Vikings: a deeper mix

The Vikings, who settled in northern England from the 800s, spoke Old Norse, a cousin of English. The two groups lived side by side as neighbours and married each other. The result went deeper than French: English borrowed everyday words like *sky, egg, knife, window* ("wind-eye"), *take, die, ugly*, and even the pronouns **they, them, their**. You borrow pronouns only when two peoples truly live together.

## Part 3. Arabic, and the road of knowledge

### Al-Andalus

In 711, armies from North Africa crossed into Spain. For nearly 800 years, parts of Spain were ruled by Muslim states, known together as **al-Andalus**, until the last one, Granada, fell in 1492. Córdoba, its capital in the 900s, was one of the largest and most educated cities in Europe, famous for its libraries.

Spanish still carries about **4,000 words** of Arabic origin (counting related forms). Many start with *al-*, the Arabic word for "the", which got stuck to the noun:
- *aceite* (oil), *azúcar* (sugar), *arroz* (rice), *almohada* (pillow), *alcalde* (mayor, from *al-qadi*, the judge)
- *ojalá* ("I hope"), from *law sha'a Allah*, "if God wills"
- Place names: *Guadalquivir* (from *wadi al-kabir*, "the great river"), *Gibraltar* (from *Jabal Tariq*, "Tariq's mountain", after the commander who landed there in 711).

### The words of science

English got Arabic words too, mostly through Latin, Spanish and Italian. Many of them are about science, maths and trade, because for several centuries the most advanced science around the Mediterranean was written in Arabic.

- **Algebra:** from *al-jabr*, "the restoring", in the title of a maths book written in Baghdad around 820 by **al-Khwarizmi**.
- **Algorithm:** from al-Khwarizmi's own name, which Latin translators wrote as *Algoritmi*. Every computer program and app runs on a word that is a 9th-century Persian scholar's name.
- **Zero** and **cipher:** both from Arabic *sifr*, "empty", which itself translated the Sanskrit (Indian) word *shunya*.
- **Alcohol:** from *al-kuhl*, the fine black powder used as eye make-up (kohl). It came to mean any very fine, pure essence, then the "essence" of wine.
- Also: *alchemy, chemistry, elixir, almanac, admiral, magazine* (a storehouse), *tariff, coffee, sugar, cotton, lemon*.

### Numbers on a long journey

The numbers you write every day — 0, 1, 2, 3... — are **Indian**. Indian mathematicians developed the place-value system with zero by around 600 CE. Scholars in Baghdad learned it and wrote about it (al-Khwarizmi again). It reached Europe through Spain and North Africa. In 1202, an Italian merchant's son, Leonardo of Pisa (Fibonacci), who'd learned it in North Africa, published a book promoting it. Europeans call them "Arabic numerals"; Arabs often call them "Indian numerals". Both are right about part of the journey.

### Chains of words

Some words have travelled across half the world, picking up a new form in each language:

- **Sugar:** Sanskrit *sharkara* → Persian *shakar* → Arabic *sukkar* → Italian *zucchero* / Old French *sucre* → English *sugar*.
- **Orange:** Sanskrit *naranga* → Persian *narang* → Arabic *naranj* → Spanish *naranja*, Italian *arancia*, French *orange* → English. In English, "a norange" became "an orange". The fruit was named first; the colour was named after the fruit.

Each step of the chain is a meeting between peoples: traders, scholars, conquerors.

## Part 4. Tea, ketchup and loot: the world inside English

### Tea by sea, cha by land

Almost every language in the world calls tea either something like **"te"** or something like **"cha"**. Both come from Chinese, from two different dialects.

- **Cha** is the word in Mandarin and Cantonese. Tea travelled **overland**, along the Silk Road, to Persia, India, Russia, Turkey and the Arab world: Hindi *chai*, Russian *chai*, Turkish *çay*, Arabic *shai*.
- **Te** is the word in the Min dialect spoken around the port of Xiamen. The Dutch, the great sea traders of the 1600s, bought their tea there and carried it **by sea** to Europe: Dutch *thee*, English *tea*, French *thé*, Spanish *té*, German *Tee*.

The exception proves the rule: Portuguese says *chá*. The Portuguese traded through Macau, where people said "cha".

Without a single written record, the word on your tongue tells you how tea reached your ancestors: by camel or by ship.

### The Americas

When Spaniards arrived in the Americas, they met plants and things Europe had never seen, and took the local names.
- From **Nahuatl** (the Aztec language): *tomato* (tomatl), *chocolate*, *avocado* (ahuacatl), *chilli* (chilli), *coyote*.
- From **Taíno** (the Caribbean islands, whose people were nearly wiped out within a few decades of Columbus): *hurricane*, *canoe*, *barbecue*, *hammock*. The words survived the people who first spoke them.

### Asia, and the British Empire

- **Ketchup:** probably from Hokkien Chinese *kê-tsiap*, a fermented fish sauce. British sailors met it in South-East Asia; English cooks made versions from mushrooms and walnuts; Americans added tomatoes in the 1800s.
- From **Hindi and other Indian languages**, during the centuries of British trade and rule in India: *shampoo* (from a word meaning "press, massage"), *pyjamas* ("leg clothing"), *bungalow*, *jungle*, *cot*, *thug*, and **loot** (from Hindi *lut*, plunder). That last one is worth thinking about: of all the words the British could take home from India, one of them meant "stolen goods".

### Hungarian: a layer cake

Every language is a layer cake, and Hungarian shows its layers clearly. Its core is **Finno-Ugric**, related to Finnish and Estonian: compare Hungarian *víz* and Finnish *vesi* (water), *kéz* and *käsi* (hand), *hal* and *kala* (fish). On top of the core:
- **Turkic** words from centuries of living near Turkic peoples before arriving in Central Europe: *alma* (apple), *búza* (wheat), *szó* (word).
- **Slavic** words from neighbours after settling in the Carpathian Basin: *asztal* (table), *király* (king, which comes through Slavic from *Karl*, the name of Charlemagne).
- **German** words from towns and trade: *polgár* (citizen, from *Bürger*), *sógor* (brother-in-law, from *Schwager*).
- **Latin** from the church and, for centuries, from law and government: *iskola* (school), *templom* (church).

And English took a word back: **coach** comes from Hungarian *kocsi*, "from Kocs", a village where a fast, light carriage was built in the 1400s.

## Part 5. The Columbian exchange

### Two worlds meet

For about 10,000 years, the Americas and the rest of the world (Europe, Africa, Asia) developed separately, with completely different plants and animals. After Columbus's voyage in 1492, they were suddenly connected. Plants, animals, people and diseases crossed the Atlantic in both directions. Historians call this the **Columbian exchange**. It changed what the whole world eats.

**From the Americas to the rest of the world:** tomato, potato, maize (corn), chocolate (cacao), chilli peppers, vanilla, peanuts, pumpkins, beans, pineapple, sunflowers, turkey, tobacco.

**From the rest of the world to the Americas:** wheat, rice, sugar cane, coffee, bananas, citrus, cattle, horses, pigs, sheep, chickens. And diseases: smallpox, measles, flu.

### National dishes that are only 300 years old

Try imagining these before 1492:
- **Italian food without tomatoes.** Tomatoes reached Italy in the 1500s but were long treated as ornamental, even suspected of being poisonous. Tomato sauce only became common in the 1700s-1800s. Pizza with tomato came later still.
- **Hungarian food without paprika.** Paprika is made from American chilli peppers, which reached Hungary in the 1500s, probably through the Ottoman Empire. It became a national flavour only in the 1800s.
- **Indian and Thai food without chillies.** Portuguese traders brought chillies to India in the 1500s. Before that, the heat came from black pepper and ginger.
- **Irish food without potatoes.** The potato became the main food of Ireland's poor in the 1700s: it grew in poor soil and fed a family on a small plot.

### The potato famine

That dependence turned deadly. From 1845, a plant disease destroyed Ireland's potato crops several years in a row. About **one million people died**, and more than a million emigrated, many to the United States. Ireland's population has still not recovered to its 1840s level.

The disease was natural. The scale of the disaster was not. During the famine, Ireland, then ruled from London, continued to **export** large amounts of grain, butter and meat to Britain, because the people who needed food couldn't pay for it and the British government believed the market should not be interfered with. Many tenants were evicted from their land. Historians still argue about how much of the blame lies with the government, but almost none say it was purely a natural disaster. (Compare the earthquake in Haiti in the science topic: hazard × vulnerability.)

### The other cargo

The biggest change of the Columbian exchange was invisible. Europeans brought diseases like smallpox, which had never existed in the Americas, so nobody there had any immunity. In many regions, epidemics killed most of the population within a century: estimates range up to 90% in some places. Historians debate the exact numbers; the destruction itself isn't in doubt. War, enslavement and hunger made it worse. It's one of the greatest population collapses in human history, and it's part of why Europeans could conquer the continent so quickly.

The horse went the other way and transformed life on the American Great Plains. Within a few generations of getting horses from the Spanish, peoples like the Comanche and Lakota became mounted hunters and powerful military forces.

## Part 6. Food also records violence

Delicious things are often soaked in history that isn't. Here are four.

### Sugar

For most of history, sugar was a rare, expensive spice. Then Europeans built huge sugar cane plantations in Brazil and the Caribbean, from the 1500s onwards. Sugar cane work was brutal: cutting cane in tropical heat, boiling it in furnaces day and night. The plantation owners filled the jobs with enslaved Africans.

Between the 1500s and the 1860s, about **12.5 million** Africans were forced onto ships across the Atlantic. About 10.7 million survived the crossing. A large share of them were taken to sugar colonies. Sugar became cheap, then a daily part of European diets: in tea, cakes and jam. The sweet taste in British tea was, for two centuries, the product of slavery.

### Nutmeg

Nutmeg once grew only on the tiny **Banda Islands**, in what is now Indonesia. In the 1600s it was worth more than its weight in gold in Europe. The Dutch East India Company (the VOC) wanted total control of the trade. In **1621**, its governor, Jan Pieterszoon Coen, led an invasion of the islands. Of roughly 15,000 Bandanese people, only about 1,000 were left in the islands afterwards: the rest were killed, starved, fled or were enslaved and deported. The Company then brought enslaved workers to run nutmeg plantations.

A famous footnote: in 1667, the Dutch and the English made a peace deal. The Dutch kept the nutmeg island of Run, which the English had claimed; the English kept a small Dutch colony they'd captured in North America: New Amsterdam, which they had renamed New York. People often say "the Dutch swapped Manhattan for nutmeg". It's simplified (each side just kept what it already held), but it shows how valuable a spice was.

### Tea and opium

In the 1700s, Britain bought enormous amounts of tea from China, paid in silver. To stop the silver drain, British traders sold **opium**, grown in British-ruled India, into China, illegally. Millions became addicted. When China's government destroyed British opium stocks in 1839, Britain went to war to force the trade open. China lost the **Opium Wars** and had to hand over **Hong Kong** in 1842. It became British until 1997.

### Bananas

In the early 1900s, US fruit companies, above all the **United Fruit Company**, owned huge areas of land in Central America, along with railways and ports. They had great influence over weak governments; the writer O. Henry invented the phrase "banana republic" for a small country run for the benefit of a fruit company. In 1954, when Guatemala's elected government started taking unused land from United Fruit to give to peasants, the US Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) helped organise a coup that overthrew it. The US government later admitted its role.

### Holding two ideas at once

None of this means trade is evil, or that you should feel guilty eating chocolate. The Columbian exchange also fed billions: maize and potatoes helped the world's population grow. Trade spread ideas, numbers and recipes. Food cultures are richer because they mixed.

Both are true: **the exchange enriched the world, and much of it was carried out through violence.** The skill is to see both, not to pick one and ignore the other.

Next time you eat, ask: where did each ingredient come from, how did it get here, and who did the work?
