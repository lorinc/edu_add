# Words and food are fossils: task sessions

Three task sessions (tasks 1, 2 and 4) and one optional task (3). Each produces something in your Google Doc.

## Task 1. Trace five words

Use Etymonline: https://www.etymonline.com

1. Pick **five** words you use every day, not from the reading. Mix them: at least one about food, one about school or work, one about technology, one you just like.
2. For each word, write:
   - its journey: each language it passed through, with the rough date, as a chain (like *sugar* in Part 3),
   - **what meeting between peoples** each step of the chain shows (trade, conquest, religion, science, fashion),
   - one thing that surprised you.
3. Check: does Etymonline say anything is uncertain, or "of unknown origin", or a popular story that isn't true? Note it.

Done: five word chains, each with the "meeting" and the surprise.

## Task 2. Trace your dinner

Choose one real meal you ate this week (write what it was).

1. List every ingredient you can identify, including spices, oil, sugar, drinks.
2. For each: where did it **originally** come from (which continent or region)? Use the reading; for anything not in it, look it up and write where you checked.
3. Pick the **two** ingredients with the most interesting story and write a paragraph on each: how did it get from its origin to your plate, and who did the work along the way (then and now)?
4. Make a simple map: a list or drawing of continents with arrows for each ingredient's journey. A photo of a hand drawing pasted in the doc is fine.

Done: ingredient list with origins, two paragraphs, the map.

## Task 3 (optional extension). Power in language

1. Find **five more** word pairs or triples (not from the reading) where English has a plain Germanic word and a fancy French or Latin one for the same idea. Example: *buy / purchase*, *help / assist / aid*. Check each one on Etymonline.
2. For each pair, write a sentence using each word. When does the fancy one get used, and by whom?
3. **Source detective:** the cow/beef story became famous through a novel, *Ivanhoe*. Answer:
   - Why might a novelist in 1819 like this story? (Scott was writing a romantic tale about Saxons and Normans for British readers.)
   - What do linguists say is true about it, and what is too neat?
   - Why do neat stories spread better than messy truths? Give one modern example from the internet.
4. Find one example from today (an advert, a company statement, a politician, a school letter) where a fancy word is used to make something sound better or more distant than the plain word would. Write the plain version.

Done: five pairs with sentences, the three source detective answers, the modern example.

## Task 4. Final piece: a museum of everyday things

Work with your sibling if you both agree to; otherwise alone. If together: plan together, but each of you writes your own labels, and puts your name on each label.

You're curating a small museum exhibition called **"History on your plate and on your tongue"**. Choose **six** exhibits: at least two words and at least two foods.

For each exhibit write a museum label of 80-120 words:
- what it is, where it came from and how it travelled,
- the meeting between peoples it records,
- one thing that is uncertain or disputed about its story,
- for at least two labels: the violence or power behind it, **and** the good that came from the same exchange (both ideas at once, as in Part 6).

End with a 100-word introduction panel for the whole exhibition: what should a visitor understand by the end?

Done: six labels plus the introduction, typed by you.
