# Sherlock Holmes: reading the evidence

English topic 1. Two short stories by Arthur Conan Doyle, from *The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes* (1892), and one piece of your own writing.

## Part 1. Before you start: Holmes's world

**When and where.** The stories are set in London in the 1880s-90s, in the reign of Queen Victoria. London was the biggest city in the world: about 5 million people, smoky, gas-lit, full of horse-drawn cabs. There were no phones in most homes, so people sent telegrams and notes by messenger. No fingerprint database, no cameras, no DNA. A detective had to notice things.

**Who.** Sherlock Holmes is a "consulting detective": people bring him puzzles the police can't solve. He lives at 221B Baker Street. Dr John Watson, his friend and flatmate, a former army doctor, tells the stories. Watson is clever, but not as observant as Holmes; he plays the role of the reader, asking the questions we'd ask.

**Why they matter.** Conan Doyle was a doctor. He based Holmes partly on one of his teachers, Dr Joseph Bell, who could tell a patient's job and history by looking at them. The Holmes stories made the idea of solving crimes by evidence and reasoning hugely popular. Real police forces later adopted methods like close examination of crime scenes.

**Reading old English.** The language is 130 years old. Some tips:
- Sentences are long. If you lose the thread, find the main verb and who's doing it.
- Don't stop at every unknown word. Guess from context, keep reading, and look up only the words that block the meaning.
- Read the dialogue as if you heard it: Holmes is quick and sarcastic, Watson is warm, clients are often nervous.

## Part 2. How Holmes thinks

Holmes calls his method "deduction". Strictly speaking, most of what he does is **inference**: reasoning from small clues to the most likely explanation.

The pattern is always the same:

1. **Observe:** notice details other people don't. ("Your right cuff is shiny for five inches.")
2. **Infer:** what's the most likely reason for this detail? ("You've been writing a lot at a desk.")
3. **Combine:** put several inferences together into one explanation that fits **all** the clues.
4. **Test:** check the explanation against new evidence. If it doesn't fit, drop it.

The crucial step is 3: one clue can have many explanations. A shiny cuff could come from writing, or from leaning on a bar every evening. Holmes is convincing when several clues point the same way.

**But Holmes is sometimes wrong, or lucky.** Stories are written backwards: the author knows the answer and plants clues that lead to it. In real life, clues are messier. In *The Blue Carbuncle*, Holmes makes a famous inference from the size of a hat. Read it carefully and ask: is that good reasoning? Some of his "deductions" are based on ideas that were popular in the 1890s and that science has since rejected. Spotting them is part of the task.

This is the skill of the topic: not just enjoying Holmes's conclusions, but **checking his reasoning**. It's the same skill you need for news, adverts and anything you read online.

## Part 3. Writing your own deduction scene (last session)

You'll write a scene of 300-500 words in which a detective (yourself, Holmes, or a character you invent) meets a stranger and works out at least **three** things about them from what they can see.

Rules:
- **Show the clues first, then the reasoning.** The reader should be able to check every inference.
- **At least one inference must be wrong**, and the detective must notice and correct it. That's what real reasoning looks like.
- Set it today, somewhere you know: a bus, a shop, a sports club. Modern clues: a phone's cracked screen, a lanyard, mud on trainers.
- Write it in the Google Doc, typed by you. Plan first (5 minutes: list the clues and inferences), then write, then reread once and fix three things.
