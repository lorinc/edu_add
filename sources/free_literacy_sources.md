# Free literacy sources

## Where to get them

- [Project Gutenberg Young Adult Fiction (Goodreads list)](https://www.goodreads.com/list/show/44192.Project_Gutenberg_Young_Adult_Fiction): young adult fiction available free as Project Gutenberg ebooks.
- Project Gutenberg (gutenberg.org): the main free ebook library.
- Standard Ebooks (standardebooks.org): the same public-domain books, carefully edited and formatted. Nicer to read than Gutenberg.
- LibriVox (librivox.org): free volunteer-read audiobooks of the same books. Good for reluctant readers, or listening while following the text.
- Internet Archive (archive.org): scans of old translations not on Gutenberg.

## Project Gutenberg author pages

## Reading ladder

Ordered by accessibility and enjoyment: start at the top to build the habit, move down for depth. Region in brackets.

Selection bar: avoid books that normalise racism, slavery or colonialism. Caveats are noted where a book is good but imperfect.

Availability: originals are public domain, but English translations carry their own copyright, so only older translations are free. **(check)** = not sure a free English version exists online.

### 1. Easy hooks: short, fast, fun

- **Arthur Conan Doyle, *The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes*** (UK). Self-contained short mysteries; one per sitting. The best entry point on this list.
- **Natsume Sōseki, *Botchan*** (Japan). Funny short novel about a hot-headed young teacher at a provincial school. Teen-relatable.
- **Horacio Quiroga, *South American Jungle Tales*** (Uruguay). Animal adventures in the jungle; very short stories.
- **H. G. Wells, *The Time Machine*** (UK). Short, gripping, raises big questions about society.
- ***The Arabian Nights*, Andrew Lang's selection** (Middle East). Aladdin, Ali Baba, Sinbad in readable form. Some tales use stereotyped Black slave characters; worth a comment.
- **Anna Sewell, *Black Beauty*** (UK). A horse's life story; plain language, strong theme of cruelty vs. kindness.
- ***Korean Tales*** (1889) and **West African folk tales** (1910s collections) (Korea, West Africa). Short folk stories. The colonial-era introductions to the African collections can be patronising; skip them.

### 2. Gripping and longer

- **Arthur Conan Doyle, *The Hound of the Baskervilles*** (UK). Full-length Holmes; the natural next step after the short stories.
- **L. M. Montgomery, *Anne of Green Gables*** (Canada). Talkative, imaginative heroine; warm and funny.
- **Frances Hodgson Burnett, *The Secret Garden*** (UK). A spoiled, lonely girl changes through a hidden garden. Contains colonial-era remarks about India from Mary early on, framed as part of her bad character.
- **Louisa May Alcott, *Little Women*** (US). Four sisters growing up; strong on character and family.
- **Robert Louis Stevenson, *Treasure Island*** (UK). The classic pirate adventure. Some sailing vocabulary, but the plot carries it.
- **Robert Louis Stevenson, *Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde*** (UK). Short, dark, a mystery with a twist.
- **H. G. Wells, *The War of the Worlds*** (UK). Alien invasion; partly a critique of colonialism from the invaded side.
- **Jules Verne, *Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea*** (France). Submarine adventure. Skip *Around the World in Eighty Days* (stereotyped portrayals).
- **Baroness Orczy, *The Scarlet Pimpernel*** (UK/Hungary). Secret identity and rescues during the French Revolution.
- **E. Nesbit, *The Railway Children*** (UK). Children coping after their father disappears. Some of her other books have dated depictions.
- **Jerome K. Jerome, *Three Men in a Boat*** (UK). Comic boat trip on the Thames; humour still works.
- **Kate Douglas Wiggin, *Rebecca of Sunnybrook Farm*** (US). Lively, Anne-like heroine.
- **Charles Dickens, *A Christmas Carol*** (UK). Short; the easiest way into Dickens.
- **Frederick Douglass, *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass*** (US). Short, powerful autobiography of escape from slavery. Pairs with history.
- **Zitkala-Ša, *American Indian Stories*** (US, Dakota). Short pieces about her childhood and the boarding schools that tried to erase her culture.
- **Rabindranath Tagore, *The Hungry Stones* and other stories** (India). Atmospheric short stories, some ghostly.
- **Rabindranath Tagore, *The Crescent Moon*** (India). Short poems about childhood; quick to read.
- ***Brazilian Tales*** (1921) (Brazil). Short stories, including Machado de Assis, Brazil's greatest novelist.
- **Ryūnosuke Akutagawa, *Rashōmon* and other stories** (Japan) **(check)**. Short, sharp, morally ambiguous; great for nuance.
- **Kahlil Gibran, *The Madman*** (Lebanon). Very short parables; read one, discuss one.

### 3. Rewarding with effort

- **Alexandre Dumas, *The Three Musketeers*** (France). Swordfights, loyalty, intrigue. Dumas's father was a Black French general.
- **Jane Austen, *Pride and Prejudice*** (UK). Sharp, funny social comedy once the style clicks.
- **Charles Dickens, *Great Expectations*** (UK). Long but strongly plotted. Skip *Oliver Twist* (antisemitic caricature).
- **Ibn Tufail, *Hayy ibn Yaqzan*** (Andalusia, 12th c.). A boy alone on an island works out the world by reasoning. Old translation is stiff; strong for systems thinking.
- **Kahlil Gibran, *The Prophet*** (Lebanon). Short reflective essays on life; dip in rather than read straight through.
- **Olaudah Equiano, *The Interesting Narrative*** (Nigeria/Britain, 1789). Kidnapped, enslaved, freed; abolitionist autobiography. 18th-century prose.
- **Kartini, *Letters of a Javanese Princess*** (Indonesia). A young Javanese woman on education, tradition and colonial rule. The best free Indonesian voice.
- **Charles W. Chesnutt, short stories** (US). Black American author, around 1900; irony and the colour line.
- **Laozi, *Tao Te Ching*, and Confucius, *Analects*** (China), Legge translations. Short sayings, deep content; good for paradoxical thinking. Dip-in reading.

### 4. Stretch

- **Alexandre Dumas, *The Count of Monte Cristo*** (France). Revenge epic; very long. Start with an abridged version.
- **José Rizal, *Noli Me Tangere*** (Philippines), translated as *The Social Cancer*. Anti-colonial novel that shaped a national movement.
- **Rabindranath Tagore, *The Home and the World*** (India). Politics, nationalism and loyalty inside a marriage.
- ***Romance of the Three Kingdoms*** (China), Brewitt-Taylor translation **(check)**. Huge historical epic of war and strategy.
- **Multatuli, *Max Havelaar*** (Netherlands/Java). Dutch author exposing colonial abuse in Java. Not an Indonesian voice, but historically important.
- **Sol Plaatje, *Mhudi*** (South Africa) **(check)**. Early novel by a Black South African writer. His *Native Life in South Africa* is non-fiction on the 1913 Land Act.
- **Thomas Mofolo, *Chaka*** (Lesotho) **(check)**. Novel about the Zulu king; ambition and power.
- **Rumi and Hafez** (Persia). Poetry in older translations; dip-in.

### Spanish-language originals

If the kids read Spanish, Gutenberg has many Spanish and Latin American originals, which widens the choice a lot.

- **José Martí, *La Edad de Oro*** (Cuba). A magazine he wrote for children; Spanish only.

### Skipped by the selection bar

- Kipling, Rider Haggard, Edgar Rice Burroughs (*Tarzan*), Hugh Lofting (*Doctor Dolittle*), Defoe (*Robinson Crusoe*), Booth Tarkington: racism built into the stories.
- Borderline: Twain's *Huckleberry Finn* (anti-racist intent, full of slurs; better read together), *Jane Eyre* (treatment of Bertha Mason), Jack London (*The Call of the Wild* is fine; his other writing isn't).

Public domain varies by country. Everyone listed died long ago, except H. G. Wells (died 1946), who is not yet free in life-plus-80 countries such as Spain.
