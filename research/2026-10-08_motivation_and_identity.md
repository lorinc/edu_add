# Motivation, habits and identity

Date: 2026-10-08. Prompted by: the autonomy ladder read as punishment-driven ("christian shaming doctrine"). Question: what does research say about fostering intrinsic motivation, building habits through new sources of fulfilment, and how habits become identity, for 13-14-year-olds?

Method: web searches for the key studies, checking each claim against a summary of the original paper. Findings marked *background* come from prior knowledge and were not checked in this session.

## Findings

### 1. Expected rewards for completing work undermine intrinsic motivation

Deci, Koestner and Ryan's meta-analysis of 128 experiments (Psychological Bulletin, 1999): expected tangible rewards for doing, completing or performing a task lowered later free-choice motivation (d = −0.40, −0.36, −0.28 respectively). Basis of self-determination theory (SDT): motivation becomes internal when three needs are met: **autonomy** (I chose this), **competence** (I'm getting good at it), **relatedness** (people I care about are with me in it).

Relevance: earning screen time back by completing schoolwork is exactly a completion-contingent tangible reward. It works as a starter, but the research predicts it will block the shift to "I do this because it's mine".

### 2. Autonomy support is a trainable adult skill, and it works

Su and Reeve's meta-analysis (2011) of 19 studies: training adults to be autonomy-supportive had an effect size of 0.63. The core behaviours (Reeve): take the learner's perspective, give a **rationale** for every request, **acknowledge negative feelings** instead of opposing them ("this reading is long, I get it"), use **invitational, non-controlling language**, offer real choices. Adults default to the opposite: requests without reasons, arguing with complaints.

### 3. Adolescents respond to status and respect, not to rules

Yeager, Dahl and Dweck (Perspectives on Psychological Science, 2018): interventions that work for children often fail with adolescents because teens are more sensitive to whether they're treated with respect and given status. What works: harnessing the desire for status, treating them more respectfully, reducing threats to status.

Bryan, Yeager et al. (PNAS, 2016), 536 eighth graders, randomised: framing healthy eating as **rebellion** against food companies that manipulate them made teens choose healthier snacks later, in an unrelated setting. The behaviour was attached to values they already had: autonomy from adult control, fighting unfairness.

Relevance: these kids' "massive ego" is the lever, not the obstacle. Self-direction can be framed as taking their attention back from companies that profit from it, which fits the practical history curriculum's "who benefits?".

### 4. Shame makes people hide; guilt about an act makes them repair

Tangney's research: shame is about the **self** ("I am bad"), and leads to hiding, denial and blaming others. Guilt is about the **act** ("I did a bad thing"), and leads to confessing, apologising, repairing. Shame-proneness in 5th grade predicted dropping out, arrest and drug use by 17.

Relevance: any system that makes a shortcut a verdict on the person (loss of status, confession rituals, "cover-up" labels) trains hiding, which is the opposite of what we want with AI use. Responses to shortcuts should target the act and its repair.

### 5. Identity language changes behaviour

Bryan et al. (Child Development, 2014), 3-6-year-olds: "be a helper" (noun) produced significantly more helping than "help" (verb); the verb wording did no better than saying nothing. Identity framing signals that the behaviour says something good about who you are. (Young children; an adult study by the same group found similar effects for "be a voter" — *background*.)

Oyserman's identity-based motivation research: when a task feels identity-congruent, difficulty is read as "this matters"; when it doesn't, as "this isn't for me". Guiding students to read difficulty as importance improved middle-school performance (Oyserman et al., 2018). The environment's norms matter: learn-through-difficulty norms help.

### 6. Habits form through repetition in a stable context; one miss doesn't reset them

Lally et al. (2010): 96 people repeating a new behaviour in the same context reached automaticity in a median of 66 days (range 18-254). **Missing a single day didn't materially affect habit formation**; only frequent inconsistency did.

Relevance: rules that reset a streak to zero after one bad day teach the opposite of the evidence. Count "most days", not "every day in a row".

### 7. Critique lands when it signals high standards and belief

Yeager et al. (2014), three randomised field experiments with 7th graders: essay feedback with one added sentence, "I'm giving you these comments because I have very high expectations and I know that you can reach them", raised the share who revised (64% vs 27% among Black students, who were most mistrustful of school); effects smaller but present for White students.

### Background (not checked this session)

- **Self-perception theory** (Bem, 1972): people infer who they are from watching what they do. Identity follows evidence of behaviour, so make the kids' own behaviour visible to them.
- **Mastery experiences** (Bandura): the strongest source of self-efficacy is succeeding at something that was hard.
- **Flow** (Csikszentmihalyi): absorption when challenge matches skill; a key experience of fulfilment in learning.
- **Retrieval practice** (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006): recalling from memory strengthens learning more than rereading. Explaining work aloud is a learning act, not just a check.
- **Implementation intentions** (Gollwitzer): "when X, I will Y" plans substantially raise follow-through.
- Growth-mindset interventions show small average effects in meta-analyses (Sisk et al., 2018); praise of process is still sensible, but not a magic lever.

## Implications for the routine

1. **Separate identity from daily support.** A permanent, earned **rank** (status, never lost) and a daily **support level** that adapts to how the day went. Support adjusts without anyone losing face.
2. **Count most days, not unbroken streaks.** Rank-ups: e.g. 5 good days out of the last 6.
3. **Respond to shortcuts with repair and diagnosis, not verdicts.** Redo the work; ask what made the shortcut attractive (too hard? too dull? didn't believe they could?); adjust the task or support. Talk about the act, never the person.
4. **Rename the vocabulary.** "Clean day", "honesty slip", "own up", "cover-up" carry moral, purity and confession overtones. Use neutral, agentic terms: "self-run day", "shortcut", "flag".
5. **Make verbal checks a teach-back:** the kid explains what they learned; it's retrieval practice and a chance to show competence. It still catches work that isn't theirs.
6. **Choices at every rank,** even the lowest: order of questions, PE activity, format of final pieces; higher ranks choose topics and help plan.
7. **Give status:** higher ranks get real responsibility (helping plan the week, teaching the sibling a topic, running their own self-check).
8. **Rationale for every rule**, written into the routine, and acknowledge feelings when work is tedious.
9. **Identity language and evidence:** the kid writes what their week shows about who they're becoming; the progress site shows totals that only grow.
10. **Phase out the screen time reward** as rank rises, so the work stops being payment for screens. (Decision for the parent.)
