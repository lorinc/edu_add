# Activities

## Source detective

**Purpose:** show that history is usually taught from the winners' point of view, and that what we read often has a hidden agenda or an indoctrination goal behind it.

**Guardrail:** the goal is discernment, not distrust. Apply the same questions to every source, including anti-colonial and anti-capitalist texts, and our own views. Not every distortion is an agenda: some is simplification, missing sources or honest error. Telling these apart is the skill. Blanket cynicism ("it's all propaganda") makes people easier to manipulate, because they fall back on whichever story they like.

Trains: humility & unlearning, paradoxical thinking, systems thinking ("who benefits?").

### The questions (use with every activity)

1. Who made this, when, and for whom?
2. What do they want me to think or do?
3. What is left out, and who doesn't get to speak?
4. Who benefits if I believe it?
5. What would the other side say?
6. What evidence would change my mind?

### 1. Old textbooks

Read a chapter from a 1900s school history on Native Americans, the Empire, or the conquest of the Americas. The bias is easy to spot. Then ask: "People back then thought this was simply true. What in your textbook will look like this to a reader in 2120?"

- H. E. Marshall, [*This Country of Ours*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3761) (1917): US history for children.
- John Bach McMaster, [*A School History of the United States*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/11313) (1897).
- Charles Dickens, [*A Child's History of England*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/699) (1851-53).

### 2. Same event, both sides

Compare what each version includes, omits, praises and blames.

- Conquest of the Americas: Columbus's own letters ([*Select Letters of Christopher Columbus*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/77820)) vs. Las Casas, [*A Brief Account of the Destruction of the Indies*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/20321).
- Boarding schools for Native Americans: Zitkala-Ša, [*American Indian Stories*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10376) vs. the "civilising" chapters in the old textbooks above.
- Opium Wars: a British telling vs. a Chinese one.
- War of 1812: US, Canadian and British textbooks each tell it differently, and each country tends to think it won. Low-stakes, a little absurd, good starter.

### 3. Wikipedia across languages

Open the same article (a colonial war, a disputed border) in English, Spanish and one more language. Compare. Then open the article's Talk page and edit history: the narrative fight happens live.

### 4. Historians disagree

Build on school work that already sets interpretations side by side (e.g. Trevor-Roper vs. A. J. P. Taylor on Hitler's aims). Ask: when did each write, what was happening then, and why might each have seen it that way?

### 5. Write both narratives

Write the same event twice: as the winners' official history, and as the losers' memory. Building a narrative is the fastest way to learn to see one.

### Counterexample to teach

The US South lost the Civil War, yet its "Lost Cause" myth dominated American textbooks for about a century. "The winners write history" is a strong tendency, not a law.

## Power maps

**Purpose:** map a power structure in a stable period, then after a transformation, and see how the system changed while the roles did not.

> "If we want things to stay as they are, things will have to change." (Lampedusa, *The Leopard*)

Trains: systems thinking, anticipatory thinking, paradoxical thinking, humility. Pairs with source detective: who wrote the story of the transformation?

### Fixed roles

Use the same roles before and after, so the comparison is fair:

- **Decides:** makes the rules.
- **Enforces:** army, police, guards.
- **Owns:** land, factories, resources.
- **Produces:** whose work creates the wealth.
- **Justifies:** church, schools, press, ideology. Explains why the arrangement is right.
- **Administers:** runs the system day to day.
- **Excluded:** has no say.

### The map

Boxes for roles, arrows for flows: orders, money, force, justification. Draw before and after side by side. Colour each role: same holder / new holder / role abolished / new role.

### Progression

1. **Fiction:** Orwell, [*Animal Farm*](https://gutenberg.net.au/ebooks01/0100011h.html). The final scene, where the animals can't tell pigs from men, is the punchline.
2. **History:**
   - France: monarchy to Napoleon. Dickens, [*A Tale of Two Cities*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/98), shows revolutionaries becoming the new oppressors.
   - Russia: Tsar to Stalin; Soviet elite to post-1991 oligarchs.
   - US South: slavery to sharecropping and Jim Crow.
   - Ghana or Kenya: colonial rule to independence, with a local elite in the same roles.
3. **Their own world:** a change of school leadership, a company takeover, a game server or Discord when the moderators change.
4. **Prediction:** a transformation happening now, e.g. the energy transition. Oil becomes lithium and cobalt: does the "extracted region" role stay, with only the region changing? Make a prediction checkable in ten years.

### Counter-questions (always ask)

Without these, the activity teaches "nothing ever changes".

- What did change for the people at the bottom (e.g. the vote, the end of legal slavery)? Why did it stick?
- Find one case where the roles themselves changed, not just their holders. What did it take?
- Why do these roles keep coming back? What jobs do they do for a society (coordination, order, taking the surplus)?

### Output

- Before/after map.
- One paragraph: what changed, what stayed, why.
- One counter-example.
- One prediction.
